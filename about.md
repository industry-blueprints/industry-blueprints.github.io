<div class="container article-layout"><div class="prose">
  <h2>The organizing model</h2>
  <p>Six components split evenly across business and technical knowledge, paired one to one: <strong>Workload</strong> (the work) maps to <strong>Reference Architecture</strong> (the landscape); <strong>Capability</strong> (the ability) maps to <strong>Solution Pattern</strong> (the approach); <strong>Use Case</strong> (the opportunity) maps to <strong>Implementation Pattern</strong> (the design). A workload normally has several capabilities and use cases; a capability is reusable across workloads when the underlying ability is substantively the same; a use case may require multiple capabilities.</p>
  <p>Everything else — AI opportunities, discovery and assessment, data, standards crosswalks, technology and product options, and governance — attaches to these six components as a cross-cutting layer, not as an additional level in the hierarchy. The knowledge base behaves like a graph: relationships are explicit, directional, and drawn from a controlled vocabulary rather than invented per page.</p>
  <h2>Bottom-up composition on the technical side</h2>
  <p>Implementation patterns compose into solution patterns; solution patterns compose into a workload's reference architecture. A solution pattern can have multiple implementation-pattern variants depending on deployment model, data availability, risk, or product ecosystem. Technology and vendor options are shown only after the vendor-neutral pattern is understood — a canonical definition never contains a vendor dependency.</p>
  <blockquote>What work do you do? → What must you be able to do? → What opportunity is worth solving? → What should the environment look like? → What approach enables it? → How can it be built?</blockquote>
  <h2>Built progressively</h2>
  <p>The library will not pretend that every topic is equally complete. Broad catalog entries establish coverage while a smaller set of lighthouse examples receive practitioner, solution, and evidence depth.</p>
