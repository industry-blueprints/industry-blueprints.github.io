## What this covers

Asset Management is not an end in itself. Public agencies manage assets because buildings,
infrastructure, land, fleet, equipment, and other physical resources enable services that people
and communities depend on.

This stage establishes that connection before examining processes, information, or technology. It
creates a shared explanation of why the capability exists, which outcomes matter, who experiences
them, and what falls within the capability's scope.

### Outputs are not outcomes

An output records that work occurred: an inspection was completed, a work order was closed, or a
project was delivered. An outcome describes the result that work helps create: reliable service,
safer facilities, accessible public spaces, or responsible use of public funds.

Both are useful, but the public purpose is clearest when asset activities and outputs can be
connected to service and community outcomes.

## How it applies to Asset Management

The purpose of Asset Management varies with the agency and portfolio. A water utility may focus on
safe and continuous water service. A transportation agency may focus on safe, reliable movement.
A parks department may focus on accessible, welcoming public spaces. A facilities organization may
focus on buildings that remain safe and ready for the programs operating within them.

Across these settings, several outcome themes recur:

- **Service reliability:** assets are available and capable when services need them.
- **Safety and wellbeing:** asset-related hazards are understood and addressed.
- **Access and inclusion:** facilities and infrastructure support the people they are intended to serve.
- **Stewardship:** public resources are cared for with a long-term view of cost and value.
- **Resilience:** important services can prepare for, withstand, and recover from disruption.
- **Public confidence:** investment choices can be explained through service needs and evidence.

A useful outcome chain makes the relationships visible:

> Clear asset information supports better maintenance and investment decisions. Those decisions
> help keep assets fit for service, which contributes to reliable and safe public services.

Asset Management contributes to these outcomes alongside policy, funding, workforce, operations,
emergency management, capital delivery, and other capabilities. The outcome map records those
contributions without claiming that Asset Management controls every result.

## Assessment

The assessment establishes how clearly the agency connects asset work to public value. Useful
questions include:

1. Which public services depend most directly on the asset portfolio?
2. Which people, communities, programs, and partner organizations experience the results?
3. Are asset objectives expressed as service and public outcomes or mainly as internal activities?
4. Is the scope clear across asset classes, organizational units, ownership arrangements, and geography?
5. Can investment and maintenance priorities be traced to stated outcomes?
6. Are important constraints—funding, policy, safety, capacity, climate, accessibility, or regulation—visible?
7. Do leaders, service owners, asset teams, finance staff, and operators describe the purpose consistently?

Useful evidence may include:

- legislation, mandates, policy, and regulatory commitments;
- strategic and service plans;
- asset management policies and plans;
- capital plans and budget narratives;
- service standards and public reporting;
- stakeholder or community commitments;
- portfolio scope and ownership records.

## Maturity progression

| Level | What it looks like | Representative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Recognized** | The agency recognizes that assets support public services, though the connection is mostly described informally. | Individual plans, local explanations, activity measures |
| **2 — Described** | Purpose, scope, stakeholders, and important outcomes are documented for major parts of the portfolio. | Asset policy, scope statements, documented outcome themes |
| **3 — Connected** | Asset objectives and measures are explicitly connected to service expectations and agency outcomes. | Outcome chains, aligned measures, service-to-asset relationships |
| **4 — Used in decisions** | Funding, maintenance, renewal, and risk decisions consistently reference the agreed outcomes. | Decision criteria, investment cases, prioritization records |
| **5 — Kept current** | Outcome relationships are reviewed as services, community needs, risks, and the portfolio change. | Review cadence, stakeholder feedback, revised maps and measures |

The appropriate target can differ across portfolios. The maturity discussion helps identify the
level of clarity needed for dependable decisions; it is not a requirement to pursue the highest
level everywhere.

## Workshop

### Purpose and outcomes workshop

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Participants** | Service and program owners, asset managers, operations, planning, finance, capital delivery, and performance staff |
| **Preparation** | Bring relevant mandates, service plans, asset plans, portfolio summaries, measures, and investment criteria |
| **Typical length** | 90–120 minutes for an initial cross-functional view |
| **Result** | A reviewed purpose statement, scope, stakeholder view, and initial outcome map |

Suggested activities:

1. Name the public services and responsibilities supported by the portfolio.
2. Identify the people and organizations that depend on those services.
3. Describe the public outcomes that matter, using plain language.
4. Connect asset conditions and decisions to service results without overstating causation.
5. Define the capability boundary, important dependencies, and known constraints.
6. Select a small set of measures that can make the outcome chain observable.
7. Record open questions, differing interpretations, and evidence that needs confirmation.

## What you will produce

The **Capability Purpose and Outcome Map** is the first component of the Asset Management
Capability Blueprint. At minimum it contains:

- a concise capability purpose statement;
- the public services and responsibilities supported;
- intended public, service, stewardship, and workforce outcomes;
- major stakeholders and outcome recipients;
- capability scope, boundaries, and dependencies;
- an outcome chain connecting asset activity to public value;
- representative measures and important constraints;
- assumptions and questions requiring later validation.

The artifact is complete enough to continue when participants can explain why the capability
exists, what it covers, and how later operating and technology choices will be evaluated.

## Worked example

An illustrative facilities portfolio might use the following starting point:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Keep public buildings safe, accessible, reliable, and ready for the services delivered within them |
| **Services supported** | Public health, libraries, administration, recreation, emergency operations, and community programs |
| **Primary outcomes** | Safe access; dependable service locations; responsible lifecycle investment; resilience during disruption |
| **Asset contribution** | Current condition information and coordinated lifecycle decisions help direct work to the facilities most important to service |
| **Measures** | Service interruptions caused by facility failure; accessibility needs addressed; critical facilities with current lifecycle plans |

This example is a starting hypothesis. An agency would refine it through its mandates, service
commitments, portfolio, evidence, and stakeholder perspectives.
