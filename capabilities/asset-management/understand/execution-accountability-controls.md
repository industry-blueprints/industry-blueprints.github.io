## What this covers

The lifecycle provides an end-to-end view of Asset Management. This stage looks inside that view
to explain how recurring work is carried out, who contributes, where accountability rests, how
important decisions are made, and which controls help the work remain consistent and explainable.

A process describes a repeatable flow of work. A role describes the contribution or authority
needed, not necessarily one job title. A decision identifies a choice that changes what happens
next. A control provides confidence that important conditions, approvals, evidence, and obligations
have been addressed.

## How it applies to Asset Management

Representative areas of execution include:

- accepting delivered assets and establishing complete operating records;
- registering, classifying, locating, and assigning stewardship for assets;
- planning inspections and recording condition consistently;
- identifying, planning, approving, scheduling, and completing maintenance work;
- evaluating service importance, criticality, risk, cost, and remaining life;
- developing capital needs and comparing maintenance, renewal, and replacement choices;
- updating records after work, projects, transfers, changes, and disposal;
- reviewing performance, exceptions, investment assumptions, and portfolio priorities.

These areas cross organizational boundaries. Service owners understand the consequences of asset
performance. Engineers and inspectors contribute technical evidence. Operations and maintenance
teams know field conditions and work history. Finance connects assets to budgets and financial
records. Capital teams create and renew assets. Executives and governing bodies make or authorize
some investment decisions.

Clear accountability does not mean that one role performs everything. It means people can see who
leads the work, who contributes evidence, who makes a decision, who gives approval, and who remains
accountable for the result.

### Decisions are part of the operating model

Important Asset Management decisions include:

- whether an asset is ready to enter service;
- what condition or performance evidence is sufficient;
- how work is prioritized and scheduled;
- whether continued maintenance, rehabilitation, replacement, or disposal offers the best path;
- which needs enter an investment plan and in what order;
- when assumptions or exceptions require review or escalation.

Recording the decision, criteria, evidence, authority, and result often provides more value than
documenting every minor activity at the same level of detail.

## Assessment

Useful questions include:

1. Are important processes connected to a lifecycle stage, service expectation, or outcome?
2. Do participants agree where work begins and ends and what a complete handoff contains?
3. Are accountability, decision authority, contribution, review, and escalation distinguishable?
4. Are decision criteria and required evidence visible for high-value or high-consequence choices?
5. Do controls support the work at the point they are needed without creating unnecessary duplication?
6. Are exceptions recorded and routed to people with the context and authority to resolve them?
7. Do completed work and decisions update the information used by later lifecycle stages?
8. Are variations across asset classes or organizational units intentional and understood?

Useful evidence may include process maps, procedures, role descriptions, delegations, approval
records, inspection standards, maintenance plans, work orders, decision criteria, investment
business cases, exception logs, quality reviews, and audit findings.

## Maturity progression

| Level | What it looks like | Representative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Recognized** | Experienced teams understand how work and decisions happen within their area. | Local practices, role knowledge, individual approvals |
| **2 — Described** | Important processes, responsibilities, decisions, and controls are documented for major portfolios. | Procedures, role maps, approval points, decision criteria |
| **3 — Connected** | Work and accountability connect across lifecycle handoffs, organizational units, and information updates. | End-to-end maps, responsibility model, shared decision records |
| **4 — Used in management** | Leaders use process, decision, control, and performance evidence to manage flow, consistency, and exceptions. | Operational reviews, control evidence, decision measures |
| **5 — Kept current** | Teams improve execution through feedback, learning, measured results, and changing service needs. | Review cadence, revised practices, improvement history |

Different processes may reasonably operate at different levels. Criticality, public impact, volume,
and the value of consistency help determine where deeper definition is useful.

## Workshop

### Execution and decision mapping workshop

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Participants** | Service owners, asset managers, inspectors, engineering, maintenance, operations, finance, capital delivery, policy or assurance staff, and people who make or support key decisions |
| **Preparation** | Select one important lifecycle segment or decision and bring its procedures, examples, records, criteria, and recent exceptions |
| **Typical length** | Two to three hours for one process and its key decisions |
| **Result** | A reviewed process, responsibility, decision, control, evidence, and handoff model |

Suggested activities:

1. Place the selected work within the service and lifecycle model.
2. Walk through a recent representative example using the work as it actually occurred.
3. Identify roles, contributions, handoffs, decisions, approvals, and escalation paths.
4. Record the information and evidence used or produced at each important point.
5. Identify controls that establish confidence, including human review and professional judgment.
6. Compare documented and observed practice and discuss meaningful variations.
7. Agree on the model, ownership, unresolved questions, and measures that can reveal how it performs.

## What you will produce

The **Process, Decision, Responsibility and Control Model** is the third component of the Asset
Management Capability Blueprint. At minimum it contains:

- the process scope and connection to lifecycle stages and outcomes;
- major activities, triggers, handoffs, and completion conditions;
- participating roles and clear accountability;
- key decisions, criteria, authority, evidence, and escalation;
- business controls and the confidence they provide;
- information used, created, updated, and retained;
- important variations, exceptions, and performance measures;
- traceability to earlier business artifacts.

The artifact is ready to carry forward when participants can use it to explain how important work
and decisions operate and what information and enabling support they require.

## Worked example

An asset renewal decision can be represented without documenting every planning activity:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Forecast condition, service, risk, or cost crosses an agreed planning threshold |
| **Participants** | Service owner, asset manager, engineering, operations, finance, capital planning |
| **Evidence** | Condition history, service impact, criticality, work and cost history, options, dependencies, funding context |
| **Decision** | Continue maintenance, rehabilitate, replace, consolidate, or retire |
| **Authority** | Recommendation and approval levels reflect cost, risk, policy, and delegated authority |
| **Controls** | Comparable options, documented assumptions, conflict review, required technical and financial review, retained rationale |
| **Result** | Approved direction, updated lifecycle plan, investment or work action, and recorded decision |

The example shows where professional judgment remains important while making its inputs,
accountability, and result visible.
