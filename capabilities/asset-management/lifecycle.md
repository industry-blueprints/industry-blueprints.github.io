## What this covers

This stage connects public services to the physical assets that support them, follows those assets
through their useful lives, and describes what successful performance means. It provides the
capability-wide view that later process, responsibility, information, and technology work can
develop in more detail.

An asset is valuable to an agency because of the service it enables. A road supports safe movement,
a treatment plant supports clean water, a public building gives programs a safe and accessible
place to operate, and a vehicle helps workers reach people and places. Understanding that
relationship keeps lifecycle decisions connected to public purpose.

## How the asset lifecycle works

Asset Management follows a physical asset throughout its useful life. Each lifecycle stage has a
different purpose, but the stages remain connected: information gathered during operation and
inspection informs maintenance, investment, renewal, and future planning.

The lifecycle is not always a straight line. An inspection may lead back to planning, a renewal
may return an asset to operation for many more years, and disposal can create the need for a
replacement.

<ol class="lifecycle-steps">
  <li>
    <h3>Plan</h3>
    <p>Identify the public service need, understand the existing portfolio, define requirements, and consider priorities and likely funding.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Acquire or create</h3>
    <p>Purchase, construct, receive, and formally place an asset into service with the information needed to operate and care for it.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Operate</h3>
    <p>Use the asset to deliver a public service while keeping its ownership, location, purpose, and operating context current.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Inspect and monitor</h3>
    <p>Observe condition, performance, safety, and service reliability through inspections, testing, monitoring, and day-to-day experience.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Maintain</h3>
    <p>Plan and complete preventive and corrective work, record what was done, and use the history to guide future care.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Renew or replace</h3>
    <p>Compare rehabilitation and replacement choices, decide when investment is timely, and return renewed or new assets to service.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Dispose</h3>
    <p>Retire, transfer, sell, or remove an asset and update the operational, financial, property, and spatial records connected to it.</p>
  </li>
</ol>

## Performance across the lifecycle

Performance combines the experience of the service with evidence about the assets supporting it.
Useful expectations commonly address:

| Perspective | Representative questions |
|---|---|
| **Service** | Is the asset available and capable when the service needs it? |
| **Safety** | Are important hazards, failures, and safeguards understood and acted upon? |
| **Condition** | Is condition known well enough to guide care and investment? |
| **Capacity** | Can the asset support current demand and reasonably anticipated change? |
| **Access** | Can the intended people use the place, infrastructure, equipment, or service it supports? |
| **Resilience** | Can important assets and services prepare for and recover from disruption? |
| **Stewardship** | Are maintenance, renewal, cost, risk, and service value considered over the asset's life? |

The useful balance varies by portfolio. The intent is not one universal score; it is a shared view
of the service expectations that lifecycle decisions need to support.

## Decisions across the lifecycle

| Stage | Representative questions |
|---|---|
| Plan | What service is needed, and what role will an asset play in delivering it? |
| Acquire or create | Does the asset meet the requirements, and is it ready to enter service? |
| Operate | How is the asset being used, and what operating limits or service expectations matter? |
| Inspect and monitor | What is its current condition, performance, and importance to service? |
| Maintain | What work is needed, when is it needed, and what priority does it have? |
| Renew or replace | Is continued maintenance, rehabilitation, or replacement the best long-term choice? |
| Dispose | When is the asset no longer needed, and how will it leave service responsibly? |

## Information carried forward

Useful asset information grows throughout the lifecycle. Keeping it connected gives planners,
operators, inspectors, maintenance teams, finance staff, and leaders a shared basis for decisions.

- **Identity and classification:** what the asset is and how it fits into the portfolio
- **Ownership and stewardship:** who is accountable for it and who cares for it
- **Location:** where it is and the place or network it supports
- **Service purpose and importance:** what public service depends on it and the effect of interruption
- **Condition and performance:** what inspections, monitoring, and operating experience show
- **Work history:** maintenance, repairs, rehabilitation, and changes completed over time
- **Cost and value:** acquisition, operating, maintenance, renewal, and financial information
- **Lifecycle outlook:** expected life, planned work, investment needs, and eventual disposition

## What this builds on

The primary input is the
[Capability Purpose and Outcome Map]({{ '/capabilities/asset-management/understand/public-purpose-outcomes/' | relative_url }}).
It identifies the services, stakeholders, outcomes, scope, and constraints that give the lifecycle
its context. Portfolio inventories, service catalogues, asset plans, maintenance strategies,
condition standards, capital plans, and performance reports provide additional evidence.

## Assessment

Useful questions include:

1. Are the public services supported by each major asset class understood?
2. Is an end-to-end lifecycle described in terms used by the people doing the work?
3. Are important handoffs—especially project acceptance, operational transfer, renewal, and disposal—visible?
4. Are service expectations connected to asset condition, capacity, availability, safety, and resilience?
5. Does information gathered during operation, inspection, and maintenance influence planning and investment?
6. Are lifecycle decisions coordinated across service owners, asset teams, operations, finance, and capital delivery?
7. Can the agency explain where lifecycle coverage or performance evidence is incomplete?

Useful evidence may include lifecycle policies, asset registers, service standards, project handover
requirements, inspection programs, maintenance histories, capital plans, disposal records, service
interruptions, and performance reports.

## Maturity progression

| Level | What it looks like | Representative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Recognized** | Teams understand important parts of the lifecycle through experience and local practice. | Local process descriptions, portfolio-specific measures |
| **2 — Described** | Major lifecycle stages, services, participants, handoffs, and performance expectations are documented. | Lifecycle diagrams, service relationships, defined measures |
| **3 — Connected** | Lifecycle stages share information and connect condition, work, risk, cost, and service performance. | Handoff requirements, shared identifiers, coordinated plans |
| **4 — Used in decisions** | Lifecycle and performance evidence consistently guides maintenance, renewal, replacement, and investment. | Decision criteria, option analyses, investment records |
| **5 — Kept current** | The lifecycle model and expectations evolve through operating evidence, service change, and completed investments. | Review cadence, feedback loops, revised lifecycle standards |

The appropriate target may differ by asset class and criticality. The maturity view helps teams
choose where greater consistency or integration would improve decisions.

## Workshop

### Service and lifecycle mapping workshop

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Participants** | Service owners, asset managers, planning, operations, inspection, maintenance, capital delivery, finance, and performance staff |
| **Preparation** | Bring the purpose and outcome map, sample asset records, lifecycle policies, service measures, handover materials, and recent investment decisions |
| **Typical length** | Two to three hours for one portfolio or representative asset class |
| **Result** | A reviewed service relationship, lifecycle, handoff, decision, information, and performance view |

Suggested activities:

1. Identify the services and outcomes supported by the selected portfolio.
2. Walk through the lifecycle using a representative asset or recent investment.
3. Name the participants, handoffs, and major decisions at each stage without mapping detailed procedures yet.
4. Identify the information created, updated, or needed as work moves through the lifecycle.
5. Connect service and asset performance expectations to the stages where they are observed or influenced.
6. Mark gaps, variations, feedback loops, and questions requiring deeper process analysis.
7. Agree on the lifecycle model and the evidence needed to refine it.

## What you will produce

The **Service, Lifecycle and Performance Model** is the second component of the Asset Management
Capability Blueprint. At minimum it contains:

- public services and outcomes supported by the asset portfolio;
- lifecycle stages with clear descriptions and boundaries;
- important participants, handoffs, and decisions at each stage;
- information created, updated, and carried forward;
- service and asset performance expectations;
- feedback loops and important lifecycle variations;
- scope assumptions, unresolved questions, and supporting evidence;
- traceability to the Capability Purpose and Outcome Map.

The artifact is ready to carry forward when people can use it to locate detailed processes,
responsibilities, information needs, and architecture requirements within the end-to-end work.

## Worked example

For a public facilities portfolio, a completed capital project might enter the lifecycle at
**Acquire or create**. Acceptance confirms that the facility supports the intended program,
establishes its durable identity and accountable owner, and transfers drawings, warranties,
equipment details, maintenance requirements, accessibility information, and financial records.

During **Operate**, **Inspect and monitor**, and **Maintain**, service interruptions, condition,
work history, cost, and user experience build the evidence used at **Renew or replace**. This makes
project handover more than a recordkeeping activity: it begins the information chain used for
decades of service and investment decisions.
