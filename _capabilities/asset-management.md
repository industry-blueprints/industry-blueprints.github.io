## Overview

Asset Management is how an agency keeps a dependable picture of the physical things it relies on
and plans for their care over time. It answers practical questions: What do we own? Where is it?
What condition is it in? How important is it to public service? What maintenance or replacement is
coming next?

Public assets include buildings, roads, bridges, water systems, vehicles, park equipment, and other
long-lived equipment. The capability connects each asset to an owner, location, condition,
service purpose, maintenance history, and lifecycle plan.

## Why it matters in the public sector

Public assets often deliver essential services for decades, while budgets and leadership priorities
change much more frequently. A shared view of the portfolio helps agencies direct limited funding
to the work that protects service, safety, and long-term value.

Agencies typically use Asset Management to maintain an inventory, schedule condition assessments,
coordinate with maintenance teams, prepare capital plans, accept assets from completed projects,
and explain investment needs to leaders and the public. It gives finance, operations, engineering,
and planning teams a common starting point for decisions.

## Personas

| Persona | How they use the capability |
|---|---|
| Asset manager | Maintains the portfolio view and coordinates lifecycle planning |
| Program or service owner | Connects asset priorities to the public service being delivered |
| Inspector or engineer | Records condition, risk, and remaining-life information |
| Maintenance planner or service worker | Uses asset history to plan and complete maintenance |
| Finance or capital planning staff | Connects asset needs to valuation, budgets, and investment plans |
| Executive or service supervisor | Reviews priorities, funding choices, and portfolio outcomes |

## Common processes

Asset Management follows the life of an asset from the first expression of need through its
eventual retirement. Planning, acquisition, operation, inspection, maintenance, renewal, and
disposal are connected parts of that lifecycle.

## Outcomes and measures

| Desired outcome | Useful measures |
|---|---|
| A trusted asset inventory | Inventory coverage; reconciliation between operational and financial records |
| Current condition information | Percentage assessed within the defined cycle; assessment coverage by asset class |
| Reliable public service | Service availability; disruptions caused by asset failure |
| Clear priorities | Critical assets with current lifecycle plans; backlog grouped by condition and service impact |
| Sustainable renewal | Planned renewal compared with forecast need; long-term funding gap |
| Effective project handover | New assets registered with complete operating and maintenance information |
| Better lifecycle value | Planned-to-reactive maintenance ratio; lifecycle cost by asset class |

The most useful set varies by portfolio. A transportation agency, water utility, parks department,
and facilities office use different condition methods while working toward the same outcome:
dependable public service supported by clear, current asset information.
