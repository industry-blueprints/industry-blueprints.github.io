## Business problem

Public-facing guidance is written from policy, by people accountable for accuracy, for readers
who are assumed to already understand the domain. The result is precise, defensible, and
unusable — so people call instead. A meaningful share of contact volume is generated by
content that technically answered the question.

## Target outcome

Published guidance that a person can read once and act on, at a defined reading level, in the
languages their community speaks, without loss of accuracy.

## Minimum maturity level: 2

**The only opportunity in this set that is viable early.** It does not require a shared case
record, a service catalogue, or a governed knowledge base — just published content and someone
accountable for it. That makes it the right first move for organizations at level 2 that want
a real result while doing the integration work that levels 3 and 4 depend on.

It also reduces demand rather than improving how demand is handled, which is the only lever
available to an organization that cannot yet measure its own resolution rate.

## Prerequisites that are usually missing

1. **A named approver per document.** Rewriting is generation; someone has to be accountable
   that the rewrite still says what the policy says.
2. **A defined reading-level target.** Without one, "plainer" is unfalsifiable.
3. **Knowing which languages actually matter.** Frequently assumed rather than derived from
   the community served or from contact data.
4. **A translation review route.** Machine translation of a rewritten document compounds two
   generation steps; the output needs a human reader fluent in the target language, not a
   round-trip check.

## How it works

Draft a plain-language version constrained to the source document, at a target reading level,
preserving every condition and qualification. Present source and draft side by side for the
accountable owner to approve. Then translate the *approved* version, with human review per
language.

Order matters: translating first and simplifying second produces divergence between language
versions that nobody notices until someone acts on the wrong one.

## The hard part

**Conditionality is where accuracy dies.** Public-sector guidance is dense with "unless,"
"except where," "if you are," and "before the deadline." Simplification pressure removes
exactly these, because they are what makes text hard to read. A rewrite that says "you can
apply online" when the source says "you can apply online unless you are renewing a licence
issued before 2019" is easier to read and actively harmful.

Instruct explicitly to preserve conditions, and review specifically for their survival. This
is the single check that matters most, and it does not happen by default.

## Human oversight

Constituent-facing content, and much of it is consequential — deadlines, eligibility,
obligations. Under
[AI disclosure and human review](/governance/ai-disclosure-and-human-review/), a named human
approves before publication.

Disclosure to the reader is **not** required here: the organization is publishing content it
has approved and stands behind, exactly as with any other drafting tool. The accountability
sits with the approver, which is the point.

## Success metrics

- Reading level of published content against target
- Contact volume for topics with published guidance (should fall)
- Repeat contacts on rewritten topics
- Coverage across the languages the community speaks
- Conditionality audit: share of sampled rewrites where every condition in the source survived

## Risk level

**Moderate to high**, and higher than it appears. The content is public, authoritative, and
consequential; errors reach many people, persist, and are acted upon without any interaction
in which a person could ask a clarifying question. Lower technical difficulty than the other
opportunities here, higher blast radius.
