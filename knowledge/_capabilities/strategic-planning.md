## Purpose

To establish where the organization intends to be in three to five years, what it will do to get
there, and what it will stop doing to make room — expressed specifically enough that a budget
decision can be tested against it.

## Desired outcomes

- Direction that survives a change of leadership, or degrades gracefully when it doesn't
- Objectives few enough to be real and specific enough to be falsifiable
- Initiatives staged so each stage delivers standalone value
- Explicit statements of what will not be done
- A line from every funded initiative to an objective, and from every objective to a measure

## Key processes

Environmental scan · stakeholder and community input · objective setting · initiative
identification and sequencing · resource and capacity assessment · plan adoption and publication ·
cascade to departmental plans · periodic review and re-baselining

## Key data

Objective hierarchy with owners · initiative portfolio with stage gates · capacity and resource
assumptions · dependency map · measure set linked to objectives · decisions to defer or stop

## Measures

| Measure | Class |
|---|---|
| Objectives with a named owner and a measure | Process |
| Funded initiatives traceable to an objective | Process |
| Initiatives stopped or deferred deliberately | Process |
| Plan objectives still current after an administration change | Outcome |
| Movement on the outcome measures the plan claimed | Outcome |

The third measure is the honest one. A portfolio in which nothing is ever stopped is a wish list.

## Level variance

- **Federal.** Statutory strategic planning and performance reporting cycles with published plans
  and annual performance reports, giving the exercise a fixed rhythm and a public record.
- **State.** Agency plans nested inside a governor's priorities, re-based when the administration
  changes — which is the most common cause of multi-year initiatives stalling at year two.
- **County / municipal.** Frequently the most genuinely community-facing planning in government,
  with public engagement built in, but constrained by a two-to-four year electoral cycle and
  limited capacity to run engagement well.

## What to get right

**Keep the objective count small.** Thirty priorities is none — the count itself is the tell of
whether the plan is real.

**Write objectives an observation could falsify.** "Improve service quality" cannot be missed, so
it cannot be managed. Compare a real measure like
[first contact resolution](/kpis/first-contact-resolution/).

**Keep a stop list alongside the plan.** Deliberately removing things as they're added keeps
capacity from being over-committed on the day the plan is adopted, catching the constraint as a
decision rather than as later slippage.

**Stage initiatives to deliver value within the horizon.** A five-year transformation under a
four-year administration needs standalone value at each stage, or it risks cancellation at the
handover with nothing delivered.

**Cascade objectives into department-specific commitments.** Departmental plans that restate the
corporate objectives verbatim create the appearance of alignment and none of the substance —
translating them into real commitments is what closes that gap.

**Link planning to the budget process.** A plan adopted in one process and a budget set in another
produces a document with no consequences. This is why the link to
[budget formulation](/capabilities/budget-formulation/) is the load-bearing one.

**Re-baseline as assumptions change.** Otherwise the plan stays fixed while reality moves, and it
quietly becomes a historical document everyone stops referring to.
