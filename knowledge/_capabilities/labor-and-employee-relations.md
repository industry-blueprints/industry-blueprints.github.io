## Purpose

To manage the organization's relationship with the unions representing its employees — negotiating
agreements, administering them, resolving grievances, and meeting the obligation to bargain over
changes to terms and conditions.

**The duty to bargain constrains operational change.** A decision to reorganize work, adopt a
system that changes how work is assigned, or alter a schedule may be a mandatory subject of
bargaining. Discovering that after announcing the change is among the most common and most
expensive process failures in public sector transformation — and it is a
[playbook](/playbooks/constituent-service-level-2-to-3/) concern, not only an HR one.

## Desired outcomes

- Bargaining obligations identified before a change is announced
- Agreements costed accurately over their full term before ratification
- Contract terms implemented in the systems that must apply them
- Grievances resolved at the lowest step, quickly
- Discipline consistent across units and supported by documentation
- A working relationship durable enough to survive a hard negotiation

## Key processes

Unit determination and recognition · bargaining preparation and costing · negotiation · tentative
agreement and ratification · impasse resolution — mediation, fact-finding, arbitration · contract
administration and interpretation · grievance intake and step processing · arbitration
representation · disciplinary consultation and representation rights · unfair labor practice
response · labor-management committee operation

## Key data

Bargaining unit definitions and membership · current agreements with expiry dates and reopener
clauses · negotiation history and settlement patterns · costing models with multi-year projections ·
grievance register by step, issue, and outcome · arbitration decisions establishing precedent ·
disciplinary actions by unit and type · past practice record

Recurring grievances on one provision indicate an ambiguity to fix at the next negotiation rather
than a dispute to keep winning.

## Level variance

- **Federal.** Bargaining scope is narrower — compensation is largely set by statute — so
  negotiation concentrates on working conditions and procedures, with a distinct federal labor
  relations framework.
- **State.** Enabling statutes that differ substantially by jurisdiction, including on whether
  particular categories may bargain at all and what impasse procedures apply.
- **County / municipal.** Multiple units in one small organization — public safety, trades,
  clerical, professional — each with its own agreement and expiry. Public safety frequently has
  binding interest arbitration, which removes the final decision from the elected body. In some
  jurisdictions negotiations occur in public session, which changes the dynamics of every
  concession.

## Where it goes wrong

**Change announced before the bargaining obligation is checked.** The initiative stops, the
organization is exposed to an unfair labor practice charge, and the union's position hardens on
the merits as well as the process.

**Settlements costed on year one.** Compounding, step progression, and the pension effect excluded,
so the actual multi-year cost is discovered in the second budget after ratification.

**Terms agreed and not implemented.** A new differential negotiated and never configured in
[payroll](/capabilities/payroll/), producing retroactive corrections and a grievance about the
correction.

**Grievances escalating by default.** No genuine attempt at step one, so volume accumulates at
arbitration where it is slowest and most expensive for both sides.

**Past practice created inadvertently.** An informal accommodation extended for years becomes an
enforceable term nobody agreed to.

**Discipline inconsistent across units.** The same conduct treated differently in two departments,
which is the argument that wins the arbitration.

**The relationship engaged only in negotiation.** No standing labor-management forum, so every
contact is adversarial and every operational change becomes a negotiation.
