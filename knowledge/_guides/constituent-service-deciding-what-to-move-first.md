Ninety minutes, right after the team has
[scored where the service actually stands](/conversations/find-where-you-are/). The room leaves
with three decisions and named owners, or the session hasn't worked.

## What this session is for

There's a [general plan for building a shared case record](/playbooks/constituent-service-level-2-to-3/)
across the whole service, and it's the right plan for most organizations. This session exists to
check whether it's the right plan for *this* one.

A service that's strong on its phone and web channels but weak on shared knowledge doesn't need
the general plan — it has one specific, nameable problem, and running the whole plan would spend
months rebuilding the parts that already work. This session finds the actual problem before
committing to a fix for it.

## Before running this

| What's needed | Why | If it's missing |
|---|---|---|
| The service has already been scored, with front-line staff in the room | Manager-only scores describe how the process is supposed to work, not how it actually runs | Reschedule — the whole session depends on the scores being real |
| The executive sponsor is there | One of today's decisions is a dispute between departments | The room produces a recommendation, not a decision |
| Department directors are there | They're the ones being asked to give something up | Objections nobody voices today surface later, at a much higher cost |
| The scores are visible on a wall | People argue with a number they can see, and quietly agree with one they can't | Everyone nods along and nothing gets decided |

## How the ninety minutes runs

| | Part | Time | What comes out of it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The lowest score, read aloud | 10 min | Agreement on the real constraint |
| 2 | Where the scores disagreed | 15 min | The actual current state, not the intended one |
| 3 | **Decide what gets fixed first** | 25 min | One thing, named |
| 4 | **Decide who owns the shared case record** | 30 min | A person's name |
| 5 | **Decide what to leave alone for now** | 10 min | A short list, with a date to revisit it |
| — | Three first moves, with owners | 10 min | Owners against dates |

Ninety minutes is tight on purpose. Given more time, this session tends to drift back into
arguing about the scores instead of deciding anything.

## 1 · Read the lowest score aloud

Read every score aloud, and stop on the lowest one.

The lowest score usually matters more than any of the others, because it tends to hold back
everything scored above it. A service with excellent phone and web channels but no shared case
record isn't really failing at its channels — it's failing at the case record, and better
channels can't fix that.

Don't debate it yet. Read it, write it down, move on.

## 2 · Discuss where the scores disagreed

Where managers scored something differently than front-line staff did is worth discussing
directly — and the front-line score is usually the more accurate one. Saying that out loud is
uncomfortable, and it's the most useful part of the session.

This usually takes one of three shapes:

- **Managers score "knowledge" higher than staff do.** Almost universal — a knowledge base
  exists, but staff don't use it because they trust a colleague more. See
  [knowledge-assisted resolution](/processes/knowledge-assisted-resolution/).
- **Managers score "routing" higher.** Routing looks fine when a request is well described. See
  [triage and routing](/processes/triage-and-routing/) for what happens when it isn't.
- **Front-line staff score something higher than managers do.** Rare, and worth stopping on — it
  usually means staff have built an informal workaround that management doesn't know exists, and
  that workaround is quietly holding something up.

## 3 · Decide what gets fixed first

**The decision:** one thing, worked on over the next two quarters.

**The rule:** fix whatever scored lowest, or state a specific reason not to. A real reason might
be a legal requirement, a contract term, or a system replacement that's already funded. "It's
harder" isn't one.

Two arguments come up almost every time:

**"Let's do everything at once."** Usually not possible — the
[case-record plan](/playbooks/constituent-service-level-2-to-3/) runs several things in parallel,
but only after the case record itself is defined first, and that first step can't be skipped.

**"Let's fix the visible one first."** The visible thing is usually the public-facing channels,
because that's what the public and elected officials actually see. It's also usually not the real
problem. Fixing the visible thing while the actual constraint stays in place just builds a nicer
front door onto the same broken hallway — and spends the budget that would have fixed the hallway.

## 4 · Decide who owns the shared case record

The longest part of the session, and the reason it exists.

**The question:** who is the one person accountable for the shared case record?

This is where most organizations get stuck for years — not because of the technology, but
because nobody was named the owner before money got spent. Naming an owner now, and accepting a
smaller shared record over a perfect one that never ships, is what actually unlocks progress.

A few rules for this part:

- **A person, not a committee**, and not a department name. A department name is the same
  problem, restated.
- **Write the name down before anyone leaves.** "We'll figure out who owns this" is the session
  failing, not succeeding.
- **Every director in the room says, out loud, whether they accept it or object to it.**
  Objections that go unspoken today tend to surface later, at a much higher cost.

If the room can't produce a name, that's the actual finding — take it to the sponsor as the one
thing blocking everything else. That's a better outcome than settling for a committee.

## 5 · Decide what to leave alone for now

Short, but don't skip it.

Set AI aside for now, in writing, with a date to come back to it. Reliable AI assistance depends
on having governed knowledge and a shared case record already in place — not on which AI gets
used. Trying to add it before those basics exist produces confident, fluent, wrong answers, at
scale.

Setting something aside without a date isn't really setting it aside — it reads as a refusal, and
it tends to come back six weeks later as a separate, unplanned purchase. Give it a date, and put
it on the wall next to the owner's name.

## What to get right

**Treat the scores as settled.** If someone disputes a number, note it and move on — today is
about what to do with the scores, not re-litigating them.

**Get a name, not a task.** "We'll work out who owns this" is worth pushing past — the session
is there specifically to produce a name.

**Give the parked list a date.** See above.

**Put a name on the case record before the room clears.** It's easy for a meeting to feel
productive because everyone agrees the case record matters, even when nobody's name has gone on
it yet. Closing that gap before people leave is what makes the agreement stick.

**Decide on AI, one way or the other.** Either pick it up or set it aside with a date — leaving
it undecided is what lets it compete for attention and budget later, unplanned.

## What to leave the room with

Four things, in this order:

1. The one thing that gets fixed first
2. A person's name, next to the case record
3. The parked list, with a date to revisit it
4. Three first moves, each with an owner and a date

Photograph the wall. That photograph is worth more than whatever deck follows it.
