Sixty minutes, in the room, with the people who run the service. The output is a
**shared, agreed description of what the organization actually operates** — a different thing
from a plan or a score, and one that almost never exists in writing before someone asks for it.

## What this session is, and what comes after it

This comes before [scoring the maturity rubric](/conversations/find-where-you-are/), and does a
different job. The rubric scores seven dimensions of the service and tells you where each one
stands, but it only works once everyone in the room agrees on what "the service" actually
covers. In constituent service, that agreement takes a session of its own to reach, because the
capability spans organizational boundaries by nature — a resident's problem rarely respects the
org chart.

Run this session first, and score the rubric second, once everyone agrees whose service is
actually being scored.

## Before the room: twenty minutes

Read the [capability](/capabilities/constituent-service-management/) — specifically
**"Where this capability ends"** and the **level variance** table for their tier. You want to
recognize a scope problem in the room, ahead of week three.

Save the AI integrations for later — there's a reason, at the end.

## Who needs to be in the room

| Who | Why | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| The person who answers the phone | Every scope answer gets checked against their Tuesday | Reschedule. Seriously. |
| A supervisor | Owns routing and escalation in practice | You will get policy, not practice |
| Whoever owns the web channel | Usually a different department, and usually surprised to be here | The channel silo stays invisible |
| Records or privacy | Retention and disclosure bind the design | Constraints arrive late and expensively |

**This session depends on the front-line representative being in the room.** The
[rubric](/maturity-rubrics/constituent-service/) makes the same point about scoring, and it
applies double here: managers describe the designed process, and what actually runs is
something else.

## Open with the boundary, not the ambition

Start with a specific, recent event, rather than "what would good look like": what happened the
last time a request came in through a channel nobody owns?

That's a concrete question about something that actually happened. It can't be answered with
strategy, and it surfaces the channel silo, the referral gap, and the ownership question in one
discussion.

## The four scope forks

Establish all four in the first twenty minutes. Each one changes the rest of the engagement,
and every one of them is a place "constituent service" quietly expands to fill the container.

| Fork | What to discuss | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergency dispatch in or out** | Does 911 touch any of this? | It shares vocabulary with this capability and almost nothing else — different standards, latency, availability, risk. Out of scope, and it must be said out loud. |
| **Status vs. determination** | Does the team answer *where is my benefit*, or decide *whether they get it*? | Answering status is this capability. [Deciding is a different one](/capabilities/eligibility-determination/), with due-process obligations attached. |
| **Individual vs. broadcast** | Is outbound public communication in this team's remit? | Tracked individual interactions here; [public information](/capabilities/public-information-and-outreach/) is a neighbouring capability. |
| **One department or the enterprise** | If this were fixed perfectly for one department, who else would still have the problem? | Determines whether the scope is a system or an operating model. |

Save the fourth for last, once the first three have built enough trust that the honest answer
comes out.

## The ownership question

There is one question that predicts more about the engagement than the rest of the session
combined:

> **"Who owns the case record today?"**

The useful answers are a name or an honest "nobody." A department name just restates the
problem. This is where most organizations get stuck for years, and the way out is always the
same: name a single owner for the shared case record before anything gets bought.

If nobody owns it, that's the engagement. Everything else follows from fixing that.

## Reading what gets said

Some phrases come up in almost every one of these sessions, and what they actually mean is
worth flagging in the room rather than taking at face value.

| What gets said | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| "We have a CRM" | A system exists. It doesn't say whether every channel actually writes to it — worth checking which ones don't. |
| "We're pretty integrated" | Worth testing against the rubric's own question: emails Monday, calls Wednesday — does Wednesday know? |
| "That goes to a different department" | The referral path is the finding. Worth discussing what happens to the person, not just the ticket. |
| "We measure handle time" | That describes the phone system, not the service. Worth asking whether problems actually got solved. |
| "We're looking at AI for this" | Note it, don't chase it yet. See below. |
| "Nobody really owns that" | The most valuable sentence anyone says all day. Write it down verbatim. |

## What the room leaves with

Three things, written on the wall, agreed out loud:

1. **A channel inventory** — every door a request can enter, counted honestly, including the
   shared mailbox someone checks.
2. **The scope line** — what is in, what is out, on all four forks.
3. **A name, or an admission**, on the case record.

Nothing else. This session sticks to those three things; recommendations come later, and holding
them until then is what keeps the room's trust in the discussion.

## When AI comes up, and why it waits

Somebody will raise it. Acknowledge it, park it, and explain why in one sentence: **guidance
that assumes a capability the organization doesn't have is worse than no guidance.**

Getting AI assistance right depends on governed knowledge and a shared case record already
being in place — not on which AI gets used. Organizations that try to add it before those basics
exist generate confident, well-formatted, wrong answers at scale. Nobody can say whether this
organization is ready until the rubric is scored, which is the next session.

Parking it credibly means committing to a date for the AI conversation, out loud, in the room.

## What to get right

**Scope by the work, not the software.** Discuss what work gets done rather than what systems
exist — that's what keeps the engagement open to more than the current tooling.

**Scope by the person's problem, not the org chart.** The person contacting government doesn't
know which department owns their problem, and shouldn't need to. Drawing the scope around the
problem, rather than the org chart, is what actually addresses the channel silo.

**Keep the room describing, not solving.** Once a fix gets proposed, people stop describing.
Holding recommendations until later is what keeps the description honest.

**Run this before the rubric.** Scoring seven dimensions works best once everyone agrees whose
service is being scored — which is exactly what this session establishes first.

**Direct questions to the representative, by name.** They're the ones whose answer matters
most here, even through a pause while they think it through.
