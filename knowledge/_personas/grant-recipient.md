A non-profit service provider, a small local government, a school district, a tribal organization,
a community group. Also a state receiving from federal, or a county receiving from a state — the
same organization is frequently recipient and awarder at once.

**This persona is first-class**, for the same reason [Constituent](/personas/constituent/) is:
a grants blueprint written only from the funder's side optimizes oversight and ignores the burden
that oversight creates, which is how programs end up funding the organizations best at applying
rather than best at delivering.

## Who this actually is

Design decisions change enormously across this range, and funders routinely design for the top of it:

- **Large, professionalized recipient.** Dedicated grants staff, an audited financial system,
  prior federal experience. Compliance is a cost, not a barrier.
- **Small non-profit.** Three to twenty staff, a bookkeeper, no grants specialist. The executive
  director writes the application at night.
- **Small local government.** A clerk or finance officer adding grant administration to an
  existing full-time job.
- **First-time applicant.** No track record, no template library, no relationship with the
  program officer, and no idea what "allocable" means.

## Goals

- Get funded, and get funded again next cycle
- Deliver the actual work — which is why the organization exists
- Stay compliant without compliance consuming delivery capacity
- Get paid in time to make payroll
- Not have to reinvent the reporting for each funder

## Pain points

- **Cash flow.** Reimbursement-based awards require fronting money the organization does not have.
  This single mechanic excludes small organizations from programs they are otherwise ideal for.
- **Application burden that selects for grant-writing capacity** rather than delivery capacity.
- **Conditions they cannot interpret.** Terms flowed down verbatim from a framework written for
  large institutions, with no plain-language guidance.
- **Every funder wants the same information differently.** Three reports, three periods, three
  category schemes, all describing the same work.
- **Indirect costs unrecovered.** Real overhead exists; recovery is capped, disallowed, or too
  complicated to claim, so the grant quietly costs the recipient money.
- **Silence, then a finding.** No contact during the period of performance, then a monitoring
  visit that identifies something that could have been corrected in month two.
- **The cliff.** Build a service, hire staff, then the funding ends and the community loses it.

## What good looks like from here

Plain-language conditions with examples. A single place to submit that remembers what was already
provided. Advance or milestone payment where the recipient cannot carry cash. Monitoring that
starts as help and only escalates to enforcement. Feedback on unsuccessful applications specific
enough to improve the next one. And a clear statement, before acceptance, of what administering
this award will actually require.

## Typical questions

- "What does this condition mean in practice, and what will you ask me to show?"
- "When do I actually get paid?"
- "Can I use the report I already produced for the other funder?"
- "Why was our application unsuccessful?"
- "What happens to the staff when this ends?"
