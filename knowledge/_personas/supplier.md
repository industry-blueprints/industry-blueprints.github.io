A national systems integrator, a local construction firm, a two-person consultancy, a
community-based service provider. Also, frequently, another government or a non-profit.

**First-class, for the same reason [Grant Recipient](/personas/grant-recipient/) and
[Constituent](/personas/constituent/) are.** A procurement blueprint written only from the buyer's
side optimizes control and ignores the cost of competing — which is how a jurisdiction ends up
with three bidders on everything and wonders why prices are high.

## Who this actually is

The range is wide and the process is usually designed for the top of it:

- **Large, experienced government supplier.** Bid teams, compliance staff, a proposal library.
  Cost of bidding is a known line item.
- **Established local business.** Competent, not specialized in public sector. Finds the process
  disproportionate and bids selectively.
- **Small or micro business.** Bidding costs real money it may not recover. Cannot carry long
  payment terms.
- **First-time bidder.** Does not know the vocabulary, the registration, or the unwritten
  expectations, and will not ask in case it counts against them.
- **Incumbent.** Knows the buyer, the environment, and the real requirement. Structurally
  advantaged, sometimes unfairly.

## Goals

- Win work that is profitable and deliverable
- Not waste money bidding for things already effectively decided
- Get paid on time
- Build a track record that helps next time
- Understand what the buyer actually needs, which the specification often does not say

## Pain points

- **Bid cost with low probability.** Preparing a serious response is expensive. If the
  specification looks written around a competitor, the rational decision is not to bid — and
  that is how competition quietly disappears.
- **Specifications that describe a product, not a need.** Cannot offer a better approach because
  the requirement has pre-decided the solution.
- **No route to ask a real question.** Pre-market engagement is restricted; formal Q&A is narrow
  and late; nobody will explain the underlying problem.
- **Registration and compliance overhead** repeated per jurisdiction, per portal.
- **Payment terms that assume a balance sheet.** Slow payment is a well-documented barrier to
  small and diverse supplier participation, and it is entirely within the buyer's control.
- **No feedback after losing.** A score and nothing actionable, so the next bid repeats the
  same mistakes.
- **Silence during the contract**, then a performance complaint that could have been raised in
  month two.

## What good looks like from here

Requirements stated as outcomes, so a supplier can propose how to meet them. Genuine pre-market
engagement, conducted transparently. Bid effort proportionate to contract value. Published Q&A
visible to everyone. Prompt payment as a policy with a measured performance figure attached.
Substantive debriefs. And a named contract manager who engages during the term rather than only
when something has gone wrong.

## Typical questions

- "What problem are you actually trying to solve?"
- "Has this already been decided?"
- "What are my realistic chances, given the incumbent?"
- "When will I actually be paid?"
- "Why did we lose, specifically?"
